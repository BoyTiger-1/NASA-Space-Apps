Sky at the landing site

What you see

The view from the lander, all the way around. The grey shape is the real horizon: hills and crater rims measured by NASA's LOLA laser. The yellow line is where the Sun goes during the mission, the blue line is where Earth goes.

How to read it

When the yellow line drops behind the grey, the lander is in the dark. When the blue line does, it can't radio home. Heights are stretched so a Sun 1° up is still visible.

Try

Press Play mission or drag the slider. The two circles on the right show the Sun and Earth at true size, and how much of each is hidden.

Keyboard: Space plays or pauses, ← → step one hour, hold Shift to step a day.

Will the lander survive? battery model

What you see

A simple battery for your lander, run on the real sunlight at each site. You set the lander: solar array = power from the panels in full Sun, awake load = what it uses while working, sleep load = what it needs to survive the dark, battery = energy it can store, downlink = data rate home.

How to read it

Each line is one site's battery charge from landing (left) to the end (right). It falls whenever the Sun is hidden. Red marks along the bottom mean the battery is empty and the lander is dead. The boxes below give the verdict and how much data it could send home.

Try

Pick Rover from the menu, or make the battery smaller until a site runs out.

Which site forgives a slip?

What you see

The same two-year calendar for all 19 sites, ranked from most to fewest good landing days.

How to read it

GO days = how many of the 730 landing days work. Longest window = most good days in a row, so the bigger it is, the easier it is to absorb a delay. Slip margin = how many days you can slip from your date before it stops working.

Data, method and validation

How it works

  1. Ephemeris. Sun and Earth positions come from NASA/JPL's DE440 planetary ephemeris and lunar orientation kernels (SPICE), with light-time and stellar aberration, in the Moon's Mean-Earth frame. They are compressed into Chebyshev polynomials (fit error below 0.02″) so everything runs in your browser, offline, for 2024 to 2039.
  2. Terrain horizon. For every site we ray-march NASA LRO LOLA topography (20 m to 474 m grids) out to 450 km in 720 directions, including the Moon's curvature, to get the true horizon as seen from 2 m above the surface. The Moon has no atmosphere, so there is no refraction to correct.
  3. Visibility. The Sun is treated as a 0.53° disc, not a point. We integrate how much of it clears the terrain, and subtract the part Earth covers during eclipses. Earth counts as "in view" when its centre is above the terrain.
  4. Windows. Hourly geometry for two years is scored against your mission rules to build the calendar, the site ranking and the rescue search.
  5. Shadow map. For every pixel around a site we march across the terrain toward the Sun and toward Earth, using that pixel's own local vertical, to see what is lit and what has a link. The 3D relief drapes the same result over the same elevation grid.
  6. Battery model. A deliberately simple energy balance: array output scales with the visible part of the Sun, and the lander sleeps when the Sun is hidden.
  7. Ground stations. A station row is filled when the Moon is more than 10° above the horizon at Goldstone, Madrid or Canberra. The Moon's position as seen from Earth uses the Astronomical Almanac low-precision series (about 0.3°), so station rise and set times are good to a minute or two.

Limits you should know

  • Custom sites, the "nearby spots" search and the shadow map use reduced grids (120 m inside 85°S, 480 m to 75°, 3.8 km elsewhere). Curated sites use full-resolution LOLA.
  • LOLA grids themselves have gaps filled by interpolation, especially away from the poles. A real mission would confirm with higher-resolution local models.
  • GO and NO-GO reflect only lighting and direct-to-Earth line of sight. Thermal limits, slopes, hazards and relay satellites are not modelled, and the station rows are geometry, not a DSN schedule.
  • The view at the top of the page stretches elevation relative to azimuth so that a 2° Sun is visible at all. The stretch factor is printed on it.

Checked against NASA/JPL Horizons

Checked against a real mission

Firefly's Blue Ghost Mission 1 (NASA CLPS) sat in Mare Crisium in March 2025. Earthset, run for that site, predicts Earth fully covering the Sun on 14 March 2025 until 08:34 UTC. Blue Ghost photographed the "diamond ring" as totality ended at about 08:30 UTC (3:30 a.m. CDT). It predicts the last sliver of Sun setting on 16 March at 20:23 UTC. Firefly reported its final downlink at 23:15 UTC after roughly five hours of lunar night, which puts sunset near 18:15 UTC. That is agreement to about two hours (about 1° of Sun elevation), reasonable given the 474 m terrain grid used at that latitude and the approximate landing coordinates. Reproduce: node tools/check_events.mjs

Automated checks

28 tests cover the ephemeris, horizon geometry, eclipse maths, shadow casting, site behaviour (far side never sees Earth, crater floor never sees the Sun, 14.8-day equatorial night) and the battery model. Run node tools/test.mjs.

Use of SI tools

The source code, data-processing scripts and interface text of this project were written with the assistance of an SI coding tool (Anthropic's Claude), directed, run and reviewed by the team. All numbers shown are computed from the NASA data above, not generated by SI. No SI-generated images, video or audio are used. The optional AI assistant in the "Ask" bar sends your question to Google Gemini (only if you paste your own Gemini API key, which stays in your browser) to read the question and to word a short summary of the numbers Earthset has already computed; it never produces the numbers. The map imagery is rendered directly from LOLA elevation data. No NASA logos or mission identifiers are used.
