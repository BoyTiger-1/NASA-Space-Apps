EARTHSET

Know when the lights — and the link — go out.

A landing-window navigator for the Moon: Sun and Earth over the real terrain horizon, for any site and any date, in seconds.

The problem

At the lunar south pole, the Sun never climbs higher than about 1.5°.

  • Every hill and crater rim decides whether a lander has power and a direct radio link to Earth.
  • Launch dates slip. When they do, planners must re-check every site and date.
  • The tools that can answer this (Quickmap, Moon Trek, JPL Horizons) are built for specialists and answer one case at a time.
2 / 7
What you do

Pick sites. Pick a date. Read the answer.

Compare up to four sites: past landings, CLPS targets, south-pole candidates, or any coordinates you click on the map. Each card gives a plain-language verdict against your mission rules.

Explorer mode explains everything for students and the public; Planner mode adds the numbers, a shareable link and CSV export.

3 / 7
The moment it matters

The launch slips 20 days. All three sites go from GO to NO-GO.

4 / 7
Rescue the mission

A later date, another site — or a better-lit spot nearby.

Earthset casts real shadows over LOLA terrain, pixel by pixel, to map where sunlight falls across the whole mission. Press Play and the shadows sweep across the ground. At the Shackleton–de Gerlache ridge, two points 80 m apart get 64% and 86% sunlight over 16 years.

NASA data makes it possible — and checkable
0.00003°

Worst difference from NASA/JPL Horizons across 26,936 Sun and Earth positions (7 sites, 2025–2038).

  • NASA/JPL DE440 + SPICE lunar orientation: where the Sun and Earth are.
  • NASA LRO LOLA laser altimetry (20 m grids at the pole): the real horizon, traced in 720 directions out to 450 km.
  • Real-mission check: for Blue Ghost's site, Earthset puts the end of the 14 Mar 2025 eclipse at 08:34 UTC; the lander photographed the “diamond ring” at about 08:30.
  • Landing coordinates from NASA, ISRO (Chandrayaan-3) and JAXA (SLIM).
