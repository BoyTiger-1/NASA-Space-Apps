Activity 1 · 25 min

Why is a "day" at the south pole so strange?

Students will explain why the Sun stays near the horizon at the lunar south pole and how terrain creates long days and long nights.

  1. Open the Earthset home page. Press Watch the next 30 days and watch the Sun's path over the ridge.
  2. Open the planner, choose the Sky tab and press Play mission.
  3. In the site list (Sites button), switch to Apollo 11 and play the mission again.

Questions

  1. How high does the Sun get at the Shackleton ridge? How high at Apollo 11?
  2. The Moon's axis is tilted only about 1.5°. Use that to explain question 1.
  3. Find a moment when the Sun is up but hidden. What is blocking it?
Answer key

1. At the ridge the Sun stays within about 2° of the horizon; at Apollo 11 it climbs to almost 90°. 2. With so little tilt, the Sun always appears close to the horizon at the poles, as it does near Earth's poles at the equinoxes. 3. Hills and crater rims: the grey horizon line. A 1° hill is enough.

Activity 2 · 25 min

Mission control: the launch just slipped

Students will use data to compare landing options and justify a decision under changing conditions.

  1. In the planner, type in the Ask box: compare the ridge, IM-2 and Malapert on 15 Jan 2027 for 14 days.
  2. Record each site's verdict, sunlight and longest night.
  3. Drag Launch slip to 20 days. Record the verdicts again.
  4. Open the Rescue tab and pick a new plan.

Questions

  1. What changed after the 20-day slip, and why?
  2. Which rescue option would you choose: a later date, another site or a spot nearby? Give two pieces of evidence.
  3. Open the If it slips tab. Which site is most forgiving of delays?
Answer key

1. All three sites go from GO to NO-GO: the Sun or Earth is behind the terrain at touchdown, or the night is too long for the rules. 2. Any choice backed by the numbers (sunlight %, longest night, Earth link) is correct. 3. The Shackleton ridge has the most GO days in the ranking.

Activity 3 · 30 min

Design challenge: where would you build a Moon base?

Students will weigh competing requirements (power, communication, water) and defend a design choice.

  1. In the planner, focus on the Shackleton ridge and open the Base camp tab. Press Find base camp spots.
  2. Compare spot 1 with your landing site: Sun + Earth time, distance to the ice, slope.
  3. Open Terrain and press Plan a drive that stays in the light at Malapert Massif.

Questions

  1. Why does a base need sunlight, Earth in view and ice nearby all at once?
  2. Spot 1 and your landing site are only a few hundred metres apart. Why are their numbers so different?
  3. Would you rather have a fixed base in the best spot, or a rover that follows the light? Argue one side.
Answer key

1. Sunlight for power, Earth for radio contact, and ice for water, oxygen and fuel. 2. With the Sun so low, small differences in height and nearby hills decide what is lit. 3. Open: a strong answer cites Earthset's numbers.

Why it works in class

Real data. Checkable answers.

Every number comes from NASA ephemeris and laser-altimeter data and matches NASA/JPL Horizons to 0.00003°. Students can press "Check it live against NASA" on the home page to see that for themselves.
